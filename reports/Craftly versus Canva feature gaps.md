# Local models can close Craftly's biggest gaps

> **Update, same day, after building the first tool.** The background remover was built and tried on a real GPU. This report's default model, BiRefNet lite, does not run on a graphics card that allows only 16 storage buffers per shader (most integrated GPUs): its graph joins up to 1,024 tensors in one operation, and every workaround failed (see [`docs/ON-DEVICE-AI.md`](../docs/ON-DEVICE-AI.md), "Why ISNet and not BiRefNet"). The app now ships ISNet general-use (Apache-2.0, 88.1 MB), which runs in about five seconds per photo on that card. Everything below was written before that test; where it says "BiRefNet lite" as the default, read "ISNet general-use". The same warning applies to any other model with very wide graph nodes: try it with `e2e/ml-probe.cjs` before planning around it.

Craftly already covers the standard single-user canvas toolset that Canva's editor offers, but it has no video or audio, no AI of any kind (no background remover, eraser, upscaler, image generator or assistant), and no shared work. The inventory gives three causes: AI, accounts and sharing are left out on purpose because they need servers; video and audio cannot exist because the document model has 8 element types and none is media; and some editor depth (pen tool, non-Latin text, group editing) is simply not built. The cheapest gaps to close are on-device image tools: four small open models total about **168 MB** and are estimated at **15 to 23 working days**, but Craftly has no Web Worker yet and the licence of the MI-GAN eraser weights is unconfirmed. Image generation should start by driving the user's own local server (stable-diffusion.cpp, MIT, or ComfyUI), estimated at **4 to 5 weeks** for a first version, because browser-only generation needs a **2.6 to 6 GB** download and a recent GPU. Video is the largest item: build on WebCodecs and Mediabunny (MPL-2.0), start with offline MP4 export, then a timeline that is estimated at **12 to 14 weeks** for an MVP and **22 to 31 weeks** for a full v1 for one developer working without AI assistance. Do not try to copy Canva's cloud AI, its 141M-asset library or real-time collaboration: Canva's own AI serving costs led it to cut its growth forecast from 30% to 20% ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/08/12/canva-startup-growth-ai-costs-revenue-forecast-by-third/)), and on 4 November 2026 it announced job cuts affecting 22% of its staff ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/04/canva-lays-off-over-1200-people-amid-slowing-growth/)), while Canva Offline and a free Affinity already remove "offline" and "free" as unique claims, which leaves verifiable privacy, no metering and open files as Craftly's edge. Several popular models are non-commercial or copyleft (RMBG, @imgly/background-removal, EdgeSAM, 4x-UltraSharp, CodeFormer, FLUX.1 Kontext, FLUX.2 klein 9B, Qwen-Image-2.1), so check every licence before you add a model. Canva facts marked † in this report are second-hand: canva.com refused direct fetching (HTTP 403) and the research agents shared one web-search allowance that ran out, so those facts come from search-tool summaries and need a check on the live site.

## Why Craftly stops at filters, and why it has no AI or video

The inventory sorts everything that is absent into three groups ([Craftly inventory note](../research_notes/Craftly%20versus%20Canva%20feature%20gaps/craftly_inventory.md), section 8). The first group is left out on purpose because it needs a server: AI, collaboration, accounts, cloud sync and publishing. The README says the last three need a server, and README and ROADMAP both place AI out of scope, with ROADMAP section 5 holding only a plan ([README](../README.md), [ROADMAP](../docs/ROADMAP.md)). The app also makes no network calls except the SVG exporter's image and font fetches, and that is the privacy claim it sells ([svg.ts:100-131](../src/export/svg.ts)). The second group sits outside the document model. `ElementType` has 8 members and none is video or audio, uploads accept images only, and the WebM export is a real-time canvas capture with no audio track ([types.ts:101](../src/core/types.ts), [assets.ts:20](../src/core/assets.ts), [video.ts](../src/export/animated/video.ts)). The third group is editor depth that nobody has built yet: vector pen and boolean operations, non-Latin text, group editing, snap to grid and pinch gestures. Only the third group is a plain backlog item; the first two are decisions or architecture changes.

Image editing stops at filters for a structural reason. Craftly stores a photo as a Blob and stores its filters as numbers, which canvas `ctx.filter` and blend overlays apply when the page is painted ([imageFilters.ts](../src/render/imageFilters.ts)). That design is cheap, non-destructive and easy to undo, but it cannot produce new pixels. A cut-out, an erased object or an upscaled photo each needs a model runtime that hands back a new image asset, and Craftly has none: layout, filters and export encoders all run on the main thread, with no Web Worker anywhere (inventory section 1, inferences). The same design depends on `ctx.filter`, which the README calls limited in Safari, so a LUT or WebGL pipeline for new tools would also remove that browser limit; that last point is my inference, not a note finding. Canva itself does not put pixel depth in its editor. Its non-AI photo tools are four confirmed Adjust sliders plus auto-adjust, filters, a blur brush, Auto Focus, Face Retouch and shadows ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/image-editor/)†), and RAW development, curves, layers and denoising live in the separate, free Affinity app ([PetaPixel](https://petapixel.com/2026/09/15/affinity-3-3-powers-up-the-free-apps-raw-photo-processing/)). So the missing pixel layers copy a product split at Canva and are not a missing basic. What Canva closes inside the editor is the group of AI cut-out, erase, grab and upscale tools, and that is where Craftly has nothing.

The two biggest absences are the ones that depend most on scale. Canva meters its AI because each generation costs real compute: Canva says its allowance limits are "operational controls and not fixed entitlements" ([AI Product Terms](https://www.canva.com/policies/ai-product-terms/)), and a price tracker reports that its free tier now allows up to 20 Standard or Premium AI uses a month, down from up to 200 Standard ([PricingSaaS](https://pricingsaas.com/companies/canva/diffs/2026W38)); Canva's own help text gives only multipliers, not absolute numbers ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/ai-access/)). A static app with no backend cannot pay that cost, so cloud generation does not fit Craftly's design. Local models move the cost to the user's own hardware, which is why the rest of this report treats model size, GPU memory and licence as first-class constraints. Video is a different problem: the cost is engineering time, not compute, and Canva's own editor is a short-form tool. Its Help described the new timeline as beta (the latest source is from June 2026, so whether that has changed is unconfirmed), and reviewers say it lacks chroma key, strong keyframing and real colour grading, though both reviewers have a commercial stake ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/creating-and-editing-videos/)†, [FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/canva-video-editor-review), [BityClips](https://bityclips.com/blog/canva-video-review-2026)). That sets a bounded target: a Craftly v1 capped near 1080p, 30 fps and a few minutes sits at Canva's own sweet spot.

## Each gap has an open route, and each route has a licence trap

This section gives one deep dive per gap you asked about. Three conventions apply. Effort figures are **estimates**, made by the note authors or by me; none comes from measured work on Craftly, and where the note author assumed infrastructure that Craftly lacks, I say so. ### Image editing: classical tools plus four small models close most of the gap

Craftly's photo tools are 21 filter presets, 8 sliders and duotone, stored as numbers and applied when the page is painted. Two kinds of work close the gap to Canva: classical pixel maths, and small neural models that run in the browser.

The classical tools are plain engineering. Canvas 2D already supports 15 blend modes through `globalCompositeOperation`, so blend modes need no library ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/CanvasRenderingContext2D/globalCompositeOperation)). Curves and levels are a 256-entry lookup table per channel built from a smooth monotone curve, applied in WebGL or in a worker. Sharpening is an unsharp mask. Healing can use OpenCV.js `inpaint`, which its default build includes, while liquify has no maintained browser library and would be built in-house ([OpenCV.js config](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/opencv/opencv/4.x/platforms/js/opencv_js.config.py), [glfx.js](https://github.com/evanw/glfx.js), [Photon](https://github.com/silvia-odwyer/photon)). The local image-editing note suggests Photopea's order of priority: Levels, Curves and Hue/Saturation first, then Spot Healing and Clone, then the Dodge, Burn and Sharpen brushes ([Photopea docs](https://www.photopea.com/learn/adjustments-filters)). Estimate from the note: about **2 to 4 days per tool**.

A low-bandwidth set (MODNet, EdgeTAM int8, MI-GAN, Real-ESRGAN in fp16) is about 51 MB; both sums are the note's arithmetic. For the runtime, the note recommends Transformers.js 4.3.2 (Apache-2.0) for Hugging Face pipelines ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/@huggingface%2ftransformers/latest)) and ONNX Runtime Web 1.31.1 (MIT) where you need custom pre- and post-processing. Transformers.js ships a runtime of about 4.7 MB, against 14.85 to 29.52 MB for the stock ONNX Runtime Web files, and has enabled WebGPU in Safari 26 and later since version 4.3.0 ([npm](https://registry.npmjs.org/onnxruntime-web/latest), [HF post](https://huggingface.co/blog/cross-origin-storage), [jsDelivr](https://data.jsdelivr.com/v1/packages/npm/onnxruntime-web@1.31.1?structure=flat), [releases](https://github.com/huggingface/transformers.js/releases)).

**Hardware and browser needs.** WebGPU is the main path: caniuse puts global support at 86.83%, but Firefox and Safari have it on by default only on some systems (Safari only on macOS 26 and later) ([caniuse data](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Fyrd/caniuse/main/features-json/webgpu.json)). Keep a WebAssembly fallback only for the smallest models. Download each model when its tool is first opened, never at start-up, and call `navigator.storage.persist()`, because Safari deletes script-written data after 7 days without user interaction when tracking prevention is on ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria)). No source gives a controlled browser benchmark for any of these models. The only timings are community reports that the note marks unverified: about 800 ms per image for full BiRefNet on a Radeon RX 9070 XT, and about 0.5 s per frame for BiRefNet_lite at 640x640 on a laptop RTX 3050 ([runes/birefnet-lite-webgpu](https://huggingface.co/runes/birefnet-lite-webgpu), [wonfy/BiRefNet-ONNX-webgpu](https://huggingface.co/wonfy/BiRefNet-ONNX-webgpu)). Run your own test on a mid-range laptop, a phone and Safari 26 before you promise any speed. For iPhones the note budgets under about 100 to 150 MB per loaded model, which is its judgement, not an Apple limit.

**Licence warnings.** Do not use RMBG-1.4 or RMBG-2.0: the open weights are non-commercial unless you sign a Bria agreement ([RMBG-2.0](https://huggingface.co/briaai/RMBG-2.0)). Do not use `@imgly/background-removal`: it is AGPL-3.0, and Craftly ships under MIT ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/imgly/background-removal-js/main/packages/web/README.md), [LICENSE](../LICENSE)); on a plain reading the note says you would have to release Craftly's source under AGPL-compatible terms or buy a licence from img.ly. Avoid EdgeSAM (NTU S-Lab licence, non-commercial) ([EdgeSAM](https://github.com/chongzhou96/EdgeSAM)), SAM 3 (custom licence and an encoder of at least 296 MB) ([SAM 3 licence](https://huggingface.co/facebook/sam3/blob/main/LICENSE)), 4x-UltraSharp (CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) ([README](https://huggingface.co/Kim2091/UltraSharp)), CodeFormer (non-commercial) ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/sczhou/CodeFormer/master/LICENSE)), and the web-realesrgan code (GPL-2.0) ([GitHub](https://github.com/xororz/web-realesrgan/)). Do not base the upscaler on UpscalerJS; it rides on TensorFlow.js, whose latest stable release is from October 2024 ([deps.dev](https://api.deps.dev/v3/systems/npm/packages/@tensorflow%2Ftfjs)). Add a credits and licences screen that lists each model and its notice. Resolve the MI-GAN weights terms with the authors, or ship LaMa instead, before a commercial release.

**Effort (estimates).** The note gives background remover 2 to 3 days, click-to-select 4 to 6, magic eraser 4 to 6, upscaler 3 to 5 and shared infrastructure 2 to 3, which totals **15 to 23 working days**. The note assumes worker and storage infrastructure already exist. Craftly has no Web Worker, so treat the total as a floor.

**Recommended approach.** Build the shared runtime first (a module worker that owns the runtime and the model cache, a model manager, the licences screen and a device check), then the classical tools, then the models in this order: background remover, upscaler, eraser, click-to-select. The remover is the cheapest and is a paid feature at Canva; the eraser and the selector share one brush-and-mask interface, so build that interface once. Leave Magic Layers alone: the notes found no open model for it. Leave generative edit to the local-server route in the next section, because instruction-edit models such as Qwen-Image-Edit-2511 (20B, Apache-2.0) and FLUX.2 klein 4B need a desktop GPU, and ONNX Runtime Web cannot load a model larger than 4 GB ([ORT docs](https://onnxruntime.ai/docs/tutorials/web/large-models.html)).

### Local image generation: drive the user's own server first, ship browser generation last

**Reaching a local server from a web page.** An HTTPS page can call a local HTTP server only with care. Chrome 142 and later asks for a `loopback-network` permission, the first call should come from a user click, and the call should use `targetAddressSpace: 'loopback'` and the address `127.0.0.1` ([Chrome guidance](https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/security/local-network-access)). Firefox 151, released 2026-05-19, is rolling out a similar prompt ([Firefox 151 notes](https://www.firefox.com/en-US/firefox/151.0/releasenotes/?redirect_source=mozilla-org)). Safari is reported to block HTTPS pages from calling HTTP localhost as mixed content, and WebKit bug 171934, which asks for loopback addresses to be exempt, is still open ([WebKit](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=171934)). So Safari users need a server with TLS (ComfyUI supports `--tls-keyfile` and `--tls-certfile`), or Craftly served from localhost, or a desktop wrapper; for Mac users on Safari the in-browser tier is the only local route that needs no extra setup. Nobody tested Safari 26 or 27 directly. Each server should get a connection card with its own CORS recipe, and Craftly should keep its output sandboxed, because a script injected into Craftly's origin could drive the user's local server after the user has given permission.

**Licence warnings.** Check the licence of every model before it becomes a preset. The SD-Turbo files that browser demos load carry a non-commercial research licence, and SDXL-Turbo is non-commercial. FLUX.1 Kontext dev, FLUX.2 dev and FLUX.2 klein 9B allow commercial use of the outputs but not of the model. Qwen-Image-2.1 is under a non-commercial Qwen Research License governed by PRC law. FLUX.3 klein and SD 3.5 are free only under revenue caps (US$5M and US$1M) and carry attribution or filtering duties, and Krea 2 is gated with no commercial terms stated. The servers have their own duties: ComfyUI and the Krita plug-in are GPL-3.0, Forge Neo and Stability Matrix are AGPL-3.0, and the Draw Things server is GPL-3.0 with a README warning against bundling client and server together ([Draw Things](https://github.com/drawthingsai/draw-things-community), [Forge Neo](https://github.com/Haoming02/sd-webui-forge-classic), [Stability Matrix](https://github.com/LykosAI/StabilityMatrix)). So talk to them as outside programs, never bundle them, and do not auto-select a non-commercial model for a project that the user marks as commercial.

**Safety and provenance.** Several licences make filtering or review a condition of use: FLUX Non-Commercial v2.1 requires either content filtering or a review of outputs before they are shown, and Krea 2 and FLUX.3 klein have similar duties ([licence text](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/black-forest-labs/flux2/main/model_licenses/LICENSE-FLUX-NON-COMMERICAL)). A local filter is bypassable, so a mandatory preview before insertion meets the filter-or-review condition better than a classifier alone. The EU AI Act asks providers of generative systems to mark outputs in a machine-readable way; Article 50 applies from 2 August 2026, and a secondary source says systems already on the market have until 2 December 2026, with inconsistent dates between trackers ([Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/), [Cobalt Legal](https://www.cobalt.legal/news-cases/digital-omnibus-on-ai/)). Whether inpaint and outpaint fall under the exception for assistive standard editing is a legal question the sources do not answer, so get advice. The note's pragmatic baseline is an AI-generated flag on the element, standard provenance metadata on export (IPTC and XMP), a licence badge per model, and deferring real C2PA signing, because a signing key cannot live safely in a static bundle and the c2pa-js documentation covers signing only for Node ([c2pa-js](https://github.com/contentauth/c2pa-js)). Canva draws the same line: it adds AI-content metadata only when a feature creates entirely new content or changes it significantly, and it forbids removing that metadata ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/background-remover/), [AI Product Terms](https://www.canva.com/policies/ai-product-terms/)).

**Recommended approach and effort (estimates).** Build one `ImageBackend` interface with a common job model (kind, parameters, seed, state, progress, result) stored in IndexedDB so history and variations are cheap, then add backends in this order. The closest open example is Krita AI Diffusion, which drives ComfyUI and offers generative fill, expand, references and a job queue ([Krita AI Diffusion](https://github.com/Acly/krita-ai-diffusion)). The mask and outpaint tools overlap with the eraser's brush interface, so build them once.

The order puts the server route first because it has the fewest moving parts and needs no flags on the user's side, ComfyUI second because it covers the most models, and the browser tier last because it is the slowest, has the heaviest download and needs a recent GPU, yet it is the only local route for Mac users on Safari. Whatever you build, tell the user that images go only to the backend they chose, never to a Craftly server, because no Craftly server exists.

### Other AI features: copy the small perceptive tools, defer the large generative ones

Canva's AI has three layers. On top sits an agentic assistant (Canva AI in April 2025, "AI 2.0" in April 2026) that calls Canva's design tools. Under it are the 2023 point tools called Magic Studio, and in-house models from Canva Research (Design Model, Craft, Brush, Lucid Origin, Proteus and an image-to-video model). Beside them are AI features in every Visual Suite format and connectors that put Canva inside Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot ([newsroom](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/canva-create-2026-ai/), [Canva Research](https://www.canva.com/newsroom/news/introducing-canva-research/), [MCP setup](https://www.canva.com/help/mcp-agent-setup/)). Canva still rents some engines: Google Veo 3 makes its video clips, and Anthropic's models power Canva Code ([FoneArena](https://www.fonearena.com/blog/456646/canva-create-a-video-clip-google-veo-3.html), [Maginative](https://www.maginative.com/article/canva-goes-all-in-on-ai-with-visual-suite-2-0-and-canva-ai/)); for many tools, such as Translate, AI Voice and music, it names no model.

For a local-first editor the useful question is how hard each tool is to copy on the user's machine. The AI note sorts the tools by type, and the line Canva itself draws (a feature that creates entirely new content, or changes it a lot, is generative; an editing tool is not) matches that sort. Perception tools are cheap to copy, hybrid tools need a small model plus a fill step, and generative tools need large weights.

Canva's pricing follows a similar split. It keeps a set of cheaper tools outside the shared AI allowance under fair use (Background Remover, Beat Sync, Enhance Voice, AI Voice, Translate, Magic Write and animations), while generation of images, designs, code, audio and Image to Video is Premium, and conversational design and Veo video are Ultra ([pricing](https://www.canva.com/pricing/), [Help](https://www.canva.com/help/ai-access/)). So copy the cheap tools that Canva leaves outside its allowance, and offer the metered kind through an engine that the user owns.

In the AI note's reading, what is distinctive about Canva's AI is not any single Magic tool. It is layered, editable output, an agent that orchestrates the tools, memory of the user's style, and the connectors. Treat that bar with care. The only independent hands-on test in the notes called AI 2.0 a very good first-draft machine, with generic-looking results and repeated prompting needed for brand style ([How Marketers AI](https://www.howmarketersai.com/p/tested-can-canvas-new-ai-20-actually)), and the notes found no general-availability announcement after the April research preview. Craftly's closest route is to let an outside agent drive the editor. The store already has 78 actions behind one `commit` path with transactions ([store.ts:118-229](../src/core/store.ts)), and the ROADMAP makes the same point; that is a ready tool surface, but this is my inference and the notes give no design or estimate. Agent control through MCP is becoming expected: Figma reports its MCP server as its fastest-growing product, with usage up three times in one quarter ([Figma 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001579878/000162828026070133/q326pressrelease.htm)).

**Recommended approach and effort (estimates).** Build the perception and hybrid image tools now; their estimate is the 15 to 23 days above. Add captions, beat markers and silence removal during the video work; the video note puts captions at 2 to 3 weeks, including the Whisper worker, model download screen and caption styles. For Whisper base the note's rough guess, from unmeasured third-party speed claims, is 75 to 120 s for a 10-minute video on WebGPU and 200 to 300 s on CPU WebAssembly; measure it yourself. Auto-reframe is 1 to 2 weeks (estimate) and not needed for v1. Send generative image work through the backends in the previous dive. For text AI, video and audio generation, and the agent, **no note supplies a local recommendation or an estimate**, so I give none; run a short research spike on local language models first.

**Licence warnings.** Do not bundle Essentia.js (AGPL-3.0) or Robust Video Matting (GPL-3.0) unless you accept putting the whole app under that licence; Craftly is MIT today. The RMBG and `@imgly/background-removal` warnings apply to video as well. Parakeet's CC-BY-4.0 licence needs attribution. The MediaPipe and Silero model licences are unconfirmed.

### Video editing: WebCodecs and Mediabunny make a short-form editor a three-to-seven-month job

Canva's editor is a multi-track timeline introduced as Video 2.0 in October 2025 ([BestMediaInfo](https://bestmediainfo.com/mediainfo/mediainfo-digital/canva-launches-creative-operating-system-with-design-ai-video-20-and-all-new-affinity-10608968)). A gapless main track holds the primary clips, tracks above it hold overlays, and a design can have up to 50 audio tracks. It supports trim, split, speed from 0.25x to 2x, crop, flip, filters, volume and fades, Beat Sync (Pro), captions, and recording. Transitions work only between main-track neighbours ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/creating-and-editing-videos/)†, [Help: audio](https://www.canva.com/help/stock-music/)†). Export is MP4 or GIF, and Free allows MP4 up to 30 minutes at 1080p while paid plans allow 2 hours at 4K ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/download-as-video/)†). Canva's own help lists causes of failed downloads: more than 10 pages, videos of about 10 minutes or more, frame rates of 60 fps or higher, and embedded YouTube videos ([Help](https://www.canva.com/help/cant-download-design-or-video/)†). The target for a first Craftly version is therefore short-form at 1080p and 30 fps. Canva documents no WebM export, so MP4 is the missing parity format, and the chroma key, keyframe and colour-grading gaps that reviewers name are gaps Canva shares ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/canva-video-editor-review)).

**Browser support.** WebCodecs runs in Chrome and Edge 94 and later, Firefox 130 and later on desktop, and Safari 26 and later, which covers 95.92% of global usage; Firefox for Android has none ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/webcodecs)). The specification is still a W3C Working Draft ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/TR/webcodecs/)). Field data from 7.7 million sessions show H.264 Baseline encoding at 99.63%, VP9 at 99.99%, AAC at 91.93% and Opus at 97.24%, but HEVC at only 77.1% and 8-bit AV1 at 87.4% ([codec analysis](https://webcodecsfundamentals.org/datasets/codec-analysis-2026/), [support table](https://webcodecsfundamentals.org/datasets/codec-support-table/)). So export H.264 with AAC, fall back to Opus where the browser has no AAC encoder, and keep HEVC and AV1 out of v1. Safari needs care: the H.264 Main and High encoder could stall with no output until Safari 27.2, hardware decoding returned B-frame video out of order until 27.0, and audio encoding exists only from 26.0 ([WebKit 27.0](https://webkit.org/blog/18325/webkit-features-for-safari-27-0/), [27.2](https://webkit.org/blog/18515/webkit-features-for-safari-27-2/)). The video note's support policy is Chromium 94 and later, Safari 26 and later (27.2 recommended), Firefox 130 and later on desktop as best effort, and the current GIF and WebM path as the fallback elsewhere.

**Building blocks.**

**Architecture and hardware.** Keep a timeline of tracks and clips, and treat Craftly's existing text, shape and sticker elements as overlay clips that the current renderer draws at time t. Use one `renderFrame(t)` for preview and export. Preview follows the audio clock (`AudioContext.currentTime`); export loops frame by frame and adds each frame with Mediabunny's `CanvasSource`, writing through a `StreamTarget`, and both handle backpressure. Decode with a small bounded frame queue (the note suggests 4 to 10 frames, extrapolated from one tool), close every `VideoFrame`, and use low-resolution proxies only for long-GOP or high-bitrate sources. Store media in the Origin Private File System, which 96.65% of users have, or keep file handles; the file picker API exists only in Chromium (30.9% global) ([caniuse FS Access](https://caniuse.com/native-filesystem-api), [caniuse OPFS](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_storagemanager_getdirectory)). A 1080p RGBA frame is about 8.3 MB and a 4K RGBA frame about 33 MB (the note's arithmetic), which is why the note caps v1 at 1080p. Craftly's renderer runs on the main thread and uses `OffscreenCanvas` only for text measurement and procedural photos (inventory), so moving export into a worker depends on fonts and DOM-dependent code; a first version can export on the main thread with bounded queues. Replace MediaRecorder for video export: its output often needs repair, which Remotion lists as a use case ([Remotion](https://www.remotion.dev/docs/webcodecs)). iPhone footage is commonly HEVC (general knowledge in the note), so expect import failures on Firefox and Edge and show a clear unsupported-codec message.

**Patents.** Use the browser's own `VideoEncoder` and `AudioEncoder`, so the encoder belongs to the browser or OS vendor. Shipping x264, x265, FDK-AAC or a WebAssembly AAC encoder would make Craftly the distributor of an implementation, where the Via LA H.264 and AAC terms and OpenH264's warning to teams that build it from source apply ([Via LA](https://www.via-la.com/licensing-2/avc-h%20264/), [OpenH264 FAQ](https://www.openh264.org/faq.html)). In 2026 Via LA added tiered streaming fees for previously unlicensed implementers; whether a free tool that only exports files is in scope is unresolved ([Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmediaglobal.com/Articles/News/Featured-News/H.264-Streaming-Fees-What-Changed-Whos-Affected-and-What-It-Means-173934.aspx)). AV1 is royalty-free under the AOMedia patent licence ([AOMedia](https://aomedia.org/license/patent-license/)). This is the note's plain reading; get a legal opinion before bundling any encoder.

The note defines an MVP of **12 to 14 weeks**: the spike, a basic media layer, one video track, one overlay track and one music track, trim and split, text and shape overlays from the design renderer, basic preview with audio sync, and 1080p MP4 export on Chromium and Safari 27.2 and later, with no captions or transitions. The note guesses that heavy AI-assisted development could cut 40 to 60% (judgement, no data behind it), which gives 9 to 19 weeks for v1 and 5 to 8 for the MVP. It also lists what to leave out of v1: an effects marketplace, 4K, collaboration, HEVC, AV1 and ProRes export, colour grading and chroma key, a keyframe graph editor, multicam, cloud rendering, AI voice and music, auto-reframe and video background removal.

**Recommended approach.** Start with the one-week spike, because it answers the two biggest unknowns, which are seek speed on long-GOP clips and Safari behaviour; the note could find no measured numbers for either. Next replace the real-time WebM recorder with an offline export of the animations Craftly already has. The renderer can already paint a page at any time t and the exporters share one timeline (inventory section 3), so a loop that paints each frame and adds it with `CanvasSource` should produce MP4 that does not need the tab in front; that step is my inference from the inventory and the Mediabunny API, not a tested result. Only then commit to the MVP. None of the open editors in the notes is a design editor with a timeline, so reusing Craftly's renderer for overlays, captions and animation is the one thing that sets a Craftly timeline apart. If you later need 4K, HEVC, ProRes or multi-hour sources, wrap the same code in Tauri or Electron with an FFmpeg sidecar, which brings the GPL, LGPL and patent duties above.

## Second-hand Canva facts and unmeasured models limit how far the numbers can be trusted

The most important limit is about Canva itself. canva.com returned HTTP 403 to every direct fetch the researchers tried (the pricing page, the Help Center and the newsroom), web.archive.org was blocked by the tool, and a reader-proxy attempt was refused by the permission system and was not retried or worked around. The research agents also shared one web-search allowance, 200 calls per turn across all agents, and it ran out part-way through each of the seven web-research notes. So the notes hold three levels of Canva evidence. The AI note read canva.com pages in the built-in browser pane on 2026-11-05, which is first-hand except for items it tags as snippet only. The core-platform note tags canva.com facts [S], meaning a search tool's summary of the page and not the page. The video-and-photo note took every canva.com citation from search summaries. ## Conclusion

The comparison changes the question. Craftly is not a Canva clone that lacks AI; it is a complete single-user editor whose missing parts fall into a closable middle and two ends that are not worth chasing. The closable middle is small: perception models of 5 to 115 MB, a deterministic MP4 export and, behind an engine that the user owns, image generation. The two ends are Canva's frontier models and its content and collaboration scale, and Canva's own 2026 shows that even Canva cannot run them cheaply. That makes Craftly's cost structure its main asset, so one test can sort the whole build order: does the feature run on the user's machine, cost you nothing per use, and carry a licence you can show inside the app?

The largest risks are facts that nobody has measured. No note holds a browser benchmark for any model, nobody tested a local server from a real HTTPS page in Safari, Canva's exact limits are second-hand, and several of the best-known models are non-commercial or copyleft, which would force a commercial agreement or a change to Craftly's MIT licence. Do the measuring, the one-week video spike and the licence manifest before the third feature, not after the tenth. The same care applies to the documents: Craftly's best claims (no network, offline, tested) can be checked, which is what separates a trusted local-first tool from a quick AI-written clone, so keep the README as accurate as the code.
